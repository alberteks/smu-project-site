**Sleepless due to social media? Investigating problematic sleep due to social media and social media sleep hygiene** (URL: [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563220302399](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563220302399))   
*(Published December 2020\)*

**Summary: “**FoMO-driven nocturnal use of social media may result in sleep disturbances and adversely influence quality of sleep” according to previous studies done on adolescents. Study investigates associations of FoMO, psychological well-being (anxiety, depression), compulsive social media use (CSMU), and sleep hygiene (habits that promote/inhibit sleep) with problematic sleep in young adults (in both academic and employment settings). Study’s results indicated that “**psychological well-being influences CSMU, which in concurrence with sleep habits, influences the association between FoMO and problematic sleep.** Significant differences existed in the strength of the association between CSMU and FoMO between \[full-time students and full-time working professionals\]. Interestingly, **FoMO is more strongly associated with CSMU among working professionals.**”

* Report on young adults in the U.K. estimated \~20% of respondents frequently awoke at night to check social media notifications which caused them to experience more exhaustion than their peers (Source: [https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0055745](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0055745))   
* Study focuses on answering three primary research questions:  
  * RQ1. What is the association between anxiety, depression, and CSMU?  
  * RQ2. Is there an association between FoMO, CSMU, sleep hygiene, and problematic sleep due to social media use?  
  * RQ3. Do the associations between FoMO, CSMU, sleep hygiene, and problematic sleep due to social media use differ for young adults who are employed versus in academic pursuit?  
* Concurrent examination of these associations for two cohorts of young adults: (i) full-time students (N1 \= 1398), and (ii) full-time working professionals (N2 \= 472\)  
* “Social media has been suggested as potentially more addictive than cigarettes or alcohol” (Source: [https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0055745](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0055745))   
  * CSMU may encourage individuals to use social media during periods of sleep latency to stay socially connected and/or avoid FoMO (Sources: [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563215301357?via%3Dihub](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563215301357?via%3Dihub), [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0277953615302458?via%3Dihub](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0277953615302458?via%3Dihub), [https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1016/j.adolescence.2018.07.009](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1016/j.adolescence.2018.07.009))    
  * A study reported that 30% of respondents used social media in the 30 min preceding their bed time and subsequently experienced disturbed sleep (Source: [https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/40/9/zsx113/3926043?guestAccessKey=](https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/40/9/zsx113/3926043?guestAccessKey=))  
* **Methods:** paper-based questionnaire/survey was conducted on a large university campus in Northern India to collect two sets of cross-sectional data. Potential participants were identified with support from the university which advertised information related to the study through notice boards in several departments. Individuals targeted for the survey were aged 18–27 years and included full-time students and working professionals. All volunteers were asked to confirm their use of Facebook. Participants were first interviewed about their SMU, sleep hygiene, & experience of problematic sleep, followed by the subsequent development & testing of scale items  
* H1. Anxiety has a positive association with CSMU.  
* H2. Depression has a positive association with CSMU.  
  * “**H1 and H2 tested the positive association of anxiety and depression with CSMU. These hypotheses were supported for both sample groups with slightly stronger relationships present for students**”  
  * Significance of H1 & H2 for working professionals contradicts findings of another study ([https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563218301420?via%3Dihub](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563218301420?via%3Dihub))   
* H3. FoMO has a positive association with CSMU.  
  * “Positive correlation of FoMO with CSMU for both students and working professionals was tested by H3. This **correlation was found to be significant for both the groups and results support findings from past studies**” ([https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0191886917302891?via%3Dihub](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0191886917302891?via%3Dihub))   
* H4. CSMU has a positive association with problematic sleep due to social media.  
  * “H4 tested the correlation of CSMU with problematic sleep. This **correlation was found to be significant among students, but insignificant among working professionals.** The significance of H4 among students **supports the theories of prior studies that social media engagement takes precedence over sleep quality or duration for students**” ([https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02673843.2016.1181557](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02673843.2016.1181557))   
* H5. CSMU has positive association with poor social media sleep hygiene.  
  * “H5 tested the association between CSMU and sleep hygiene and was **found to be significant for both students and working professionals.** The findings are consistent with previous studies which suggest that CSMU may adversely influence users' sleep behaviors (or sleep hygiene)” (Sources: [https://www.pediatricnursing.org/article/S0882-5963(16)30035-5/fulltext](https://www.pediatricnursing.org/article/S0882-5963\(16\)30035-5/fulltext), [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563220302399\#bib82](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563220302399#bib82))   
* H6. FoMO has positive association with problematic sleep due to social media.  
  * “H6 tested the association between FoMO and problematic sleep. **No significant association existed for both samples. This finding may be attributed to disparate motivational, personal, or contextual factors that may drive FoMO**”  
  * “The mediation analysis confirmed that CSMU partially mediated the association between FoMO and sleep hygiene for both samples, whereas sleep hygiene partially mediated the relationship between CSMU and problematic sleep for both sample groups. Furthermore, CSMU and sleep hygiene fully mediated the association of FoMO and problematic sleep, as the direct effect of FoMO on problematic sleep became insignificant on addition of mediators. These results suggest that sleep hygiene plays a definitive role in the development of problematic sleep patterns among working professionals and students”  
* H7. FoMO has a positive association with poor social media sleep hygiene.  
  * “H7 tested the association between FoMO and sleep hygiene. This **association was significant for students, which is supported by prior research on effects of FoMO on young social media users** (e.g. [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1389945719313784?via%3Dihub](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1389945719313784?via%3Dihub); [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563220302399\#bib73](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563220302399#bib73)). **This suggests that students give considerable value to FoMO and may consequently experience higher levels of nomophobia**”  
* H8. Poor social media sleep hygiene has a positive association with problematic sleep due to social media.  
  * “H8 determined that **sleep hygiene was positively associated with problematic sleep due to social media use for both students and working professionals. This finding is consistent with prior studies** which suggest that sleep hygiene can influence the quality and duration of sleep experienced by social media users” ([https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1089/cyber.2012.0157](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1089/cyber.2012.0157), [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563220302399\#bib52](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563220302399#bib52))   
  * “This means that **individuals who use social media during periods of sleep latency may be at risk of experiencing adverse changes in physiological functions, or other probable health-related issues that can arise due to problematic sleep patterns**. This finding indicates that **maintaining appropriate sleep hygiene practices, despite nocturnal social media use, is critical for young adults** (both students and working professionals) to avoid experiencing problematic sleep”   
* **Limitations:** self-reported data introduces bias; data collection occurred in specific region of a single country, potentially limiting applicability of study; majority of respondents in both data sets were female; convenience sampling used for data collection; due to self-reported cross-sectional survey, validity of findings may be affected by social desirability bias due to lack of respondents’ truthfulness/hesitation in reporting personal sleep-related habits; findings are currently limited on insights gained from a single social media platform (Facebook)

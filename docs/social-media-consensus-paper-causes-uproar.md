**Social media consensus paper causes social media uproar** (URL: [https://www.science.org/content/article/social-media-consensus-paper-causes-social-media-uproar](https://www.science.org/content/article/social-media-consensus-paper-causes-social-media-uproar)) 

*(Published May 2025\)*

* Preprint reporting an “expert consensus” on key questions about teen smartphone use and mental health under heavy fire on social media  
    * Critics say the paper has severe weaknesses and the evidence in the field is too thin to support consensus  
        * “I don’t think we’re at a point with the research where it’s worthwhile or useful to try and form consensus statements” – Pete Etchells, Bath Spa University  
* Paper was a response to polarized public debate, senior author of paper claims  
    * Some prominent researchers argue social media & smartphone use are severely detrimental to teen mental health, whereas others argue the concern is moral panic w/ no supporting evidence  
    * Senior author \+ coauthors wanted to establish whether majority of researchers held more nuanced views, & whether common ground could be found among opposing factions  
* Team first developed a list of 26 claims, including that “heavy daily use of smartphones and social media can cause attention fragmentation” and “social media increases perfectionism among adolescent girls”  
    * Derived from claims made in book *The Anxious Generation: How the Great Rewiring of Childhood Is Causing an Epidemic of Mental Illness* by NYU social psychologist Jonathan Haidt (who also authored the paper)  
* In initial survey, 100+ invited experts stated their level of belief in each claim, as well as their understanding of the strength of evidence supporting each claim  
    * Based on responses, team revised all claims into “consensus statement”  
    * End result \= list of 26 statements that were each rated as accurate or somewhat accurate by more than 92% of experts  
        * 95% of panel agreed that “there is some evidence that screen time has increased and time with friends in person has decreased, especially in the U.S.” whereas 97% agreed that “the evidence regarding the effect of attention fragmentation on mental health is preliminary”  
    * Consensus statements should not be seen as an “arrival point,” says the first author of the paper, but as a starting point for future research  
    * The paper “defined a set of experts arbitrarily” then cast anyone who disagrees as being outside the consensus. “That is the perfect way to kill debate in an area where we need more nuance,” critic says  
    * Others worry some experts may have responded to questions outside their area of expertise, even though they were able to opt out of any question   
        * For instance, in initial survey, several respondents claimed there was strong, replicated evidence that social media causes girls to compare their appearance w/ others, even though no such studies appear to exist  
* Much of the criticism of the preprint centers around how the findings have been communicated, both in the paper and on social media   
    * Co-author of preprint critical of the framing: believes there’s been too much emphasis on the survey of experts that occurred before the consensus-building process   
        * That initial process rested on “snap judgements,” he says, and used much stronger statements  
        * In contrast, he says, the final consensus statements “mostly paint a picture of a lack of evidence”  
        * (Title and abstract of preprint both suggest otherwise, however. For example, it omits caveats included in actual statements – abstract says experts agreed that “heavy smartphone and SMU can cause sleep problems,” but the consensus statement emphasized that “the extent to which it causes sleep deprivation specifically remains unclear”  
    * Paper overall shows strong agreement on weak statements

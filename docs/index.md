# Purpose

### What is this module, and who is it intended for?

This is an educational module synthesizing takeaways from (as of 2026\) existing scientific research on social media use in adolescents. It also includes information from relevant books, surveys from reputable polling organizations, and news articles/advisories. 

We categorized impacts and information about social media into the following categories: interpersonal relationships, fear-of-missing-out and procrastination, neuroscience on social media, sleep, academic performance, body image & eating disorders, behavioral impacts, and information for parents and educators. We urge you to navigate this module however best suits your personal experiences and/or interests around social media. Information is not sequential, so feel free to jump around sections – no relevant information will be missed.

This module is intended for anyone interested in the way social media use impacts the lives of adolescents. Our goal is to also provide actionable strategies for forming habits and guidelines around social media use that align with healthy digital wellbeing; social media is a very powerful tool, and at the same time a complex one which has both benefits and drawbacks. By informing ourselves around potential struggles and strategies to avoid these struggles while staying connected through social media, we hope to maximize the benefits and reduce the drawbacks. 

We would also like to note some changes to the way we use social media are dictated not only by individual choices, but also by the companies which create the social media services. The aim of this module is not necessarily to advocate for changes there; instead, it is to provide information and potential strategies on an individual level. However, we do recognize that there are potential issues fundamental to the way social media is structured, and this is an important advocacy topic to pursue in the future – especially as social media services evolve and continue gaining popularity. 

### SNS use in teens: stats and questions 

- **Nearly all teens** – 96% – **reported using the internet daily** in a Pew Research survey from 2024[^1]  
    - Furthermore, nearly half of teens report being online “almost constantly,” a number which has increased by over 25% over the previous decade[^1]  
        - For a specific number, **teens report an average screen time of 7 hours and 22 minutes per day, not including** computer use for **schoolwork**[^2]  
- Out of this time spent online, a significant proportion of teens use social media/social networking services  
    - **U.S. teens average 4.8 hours on seven popular social media apps** (including YouTube, Instagram, & TikTok, the three most popular)[^3]  
        - 37% report 5+ hrs/day, 14% report 4-5 hrs/day, 26% report 2-4 hrs/day, and 23% report <2 hrs/day[^3]  
    - Adolescents also often visit their preferred social media site 100+ times/day[^4]  
- **Given the large extent** (and rise over the previous couple decades) **of social media use among adolescents, it is important to investigate the positive and negative impacts of social media in this age group. This can inform best practices for a healthy balance of using digital tools to stay connected and learn more about the world while avoiding potential behavioral issues** like procrastination, body image issues, or others.   
    - It is imperative to investigate a variety of resources since so many factors can regulate the impact of SNS for each individual. Only from a wide variety of resources and population-specific investigation can we begin to form conclusions about broader trends.  
        - This is highlighted by the lack of consensus among the scientific community around social media’s impact. Further investigation is still needed (and some major studies have faced controversy around their conclusions)[^5]. That being said, a number of individuals (policymakers, authors, parents, and teens) have concerns given this lack of clear information, and it is important to address these concerns to make the best of the digital age we live in for ourselves and the communities around us.

### Moment of reflection

While you have the opportunity, we urge you to take a moment to reflect on your own habits and opinions around social media use. Identifying the areas that interest and/or concern you most about it can help focus your attention on the most relevant information for your own life. 

At the same time, please note that preconceived notions about social media’s impacts, or peer pressure around our opinions on social media, can impact our perception of our own experiences. To that end, we recommend that you consider skimming through information in multiple sections, if they feel at all relevant to your use.

For your convenience, the most **important takeaways** in each section **are bolded** to help quick navigation. Similarly, concrete *strategies for maximizing digital wellbeing* in each category *are italicized*.

# Interpersonal Relationships

### Statistics on social media’s role in friendships

- **Social media can be a way to form new friendships**  
    - 62% of teens report sharing their social media username w/ a new friend as a way to stay in touch[^6]  
- **One of social media’s primary uses for teens is staying in touch with friends**  
    - 94% of teens say they spend time with friends in general on social media[^6]  
    - 30% of teens say they spend time w/ friends on social media every day[^6]  
    - Social media services are, for most (66%) teens, the top way in which they report communicating w/ friends[^6]  
    - In one study, around half of adolescents’ use of Instagram and Snapchat involved staying in touch with their friends[^7]  
- **Social media is potentially a preferred method of communication for teens than traditional messaging apps**  
    - In one study, “although WhatsApp was used by the largest group of participants, they used WhatsApp relatively less frequently with close friends than Instagram or Snapchat”[^7]

### What qualities of friendships are present online vs. in person

- Qualities of friendships can be (approximately) evaluated via six criteria: self-disclosure, validation, companionship, instrumental support, conflict, & conflict resolution[^8]  
    - Studies found that **self-disclosure (namely, seeking support through vulnerability) via technology-mediated communication can reduce negative emotions–just like in-person interactions**[^8]  
        - Analysis of adolescents’ use of Snapchat also showed “mundane” information is regularly shared between users, drawing parallels to the nature of in-person interaction & self-presentation[^8]  
    - **One category in which social media can actually be beneficial for friendships over in-person interaction is validation**  
        - Through mediums such as “Likes” and commenting, teens can seek reassurance from friends w/o having to overtly ask for feedback  
        - In focus groups, some adolescents reported liking posts as a form of “virtual gift-giving” (Are the Qualities of Adolescents’ Offline Friendships Present in Digital Interactions?)  
    - **There is a notable difference in companionship between in-person and online interaction in that online mediums lack face-to-face expressions**  
        - However, the instant nature of many SNS platforms means that many still report feeling close to their friends while messaging  
    - **Examples of teens supporting one another on online networks include supporting their friends if they are victims of cyberbullying and asking for help with offline tasks such as homework**  
    - **Conflict and conflict resolution online has some qualities of in-person friendships and some differences as well; sources of conflict, especially undependability and betrayal/spreading rumors, are similar to in-person interactions. Some teens report preferring to resolve conflicts on social media due to less of a confrontational feeling, while others report preferring to resolve them in-person**[^8]**.**

### Social feedback & identity reinforcement via SNS

- **Peer pressure is a significant factor in what social media posts receive many (or few) likes, as well as a reason for individual teens to like (or not like) certain content**  
- When shown photos to like or move on from, teens were significantly more likely (than expected by chance) to match their peers’ opinions[^9]  
    - Meaning, if a photo already had many likes, teens were more probable to like it as well–even if they might otherwise judge the photo’s actual content differently  
- **Adolescents using social media may struggle to examine some “risky” behaviors holistically, as a result of susceptibility to peer pressure**   
- Adolescent tendencies to match their peers in liking (or not liking) photos hold true even when photos exhibited behaviors researchers categorized as “risky”[^9]  
    - In fact, “participants demonstrated significantly less activation in a network of regions implicated in cognitive control and response inhibition**”** when viewing these photos depicting “risky” behaviors[^9]  
        - Shows that peer pressure may override judgements teens could otherwise make about what behaviors to support or stay away from on social media  
- [Neural sensitivity to social reward/punishment anticipation](association-of-habitual-checking-behaviors.md)


- **Another way social media services can impact users’ social behavior and self-perception is through identity reinforcement, as proposed by Social Identity Gratifications theory (SIG)**  
    - SIG suggests that we strive to maintain social group affiliations (race, ethnicity, etc.) as a core component of our personal identities[^10]  
    - In order to reinforce these affiliations, social media users may seek out media featuring others w/ the same racial/ethnic identities[^10]  
    - This can have both positive and negative impacts on self-concept, depending on the media available/accessed as well as the user’s self-comparison assessment to the “media models” they find   
    - Social media use can damage self-concept if users encounter stereotypical media portrayals of underrepresented minority groups they identify with, hate speech or microaggressions toward these groups, or a general lack of media representation of certain groups (indicating to the user that society values certain groups less than others)[^10]  
    - Social media can positively reinforce self-concept if, based on the SIG theory, people find role models with similar identities (and characteristics) to themselves, who are proud of their own identities and share them outwardly with others[^10]

### Perceived friendship closeness on SNS vs in-person

- **For many teens, social media is a way of increasing connection experienced in friendships**  
    - 83% of teens using social media state it makes them more connected to information about what is happening in their friends’ lives[^6]  
    - 70% of teens using social media state it makes them better connected to their friends’ feelings[^6]  
- **Teens w/ access to smartphones that use social media are more likely to report feeling “a lot” more connecting to what is happening in their friends’ lives, compared to teens w/o smartphones**  
    - 36% of smartphone owners report feeling “a lot” more connected to friends, compared to 25% of teens w/o smartphone access[^6]  
- **Different social media platforms have different impacts on perceived friendship closeness over time**  
    - One study found adolescents using WhatsApp and Instagram with their close friends over the course of three weeks improved their self-reported feeling of friendship closeness. However, the same was not true for Snapchat use, where there was no significant impact on friendship closeness–in the short or long term[^7]   
- **The impact of social media on friendship closeness can be very dependent on the individual (past experiences, ways of using social media, etc.)**  
    - In a study examining adolescent’s WhatsApp, Instagram, and Snapchat use over three weeks, researchers found “significant heterogeneity” in the results of how social media use impacted friendship closeness[^7].   
        - This indicates that person-to-person impacts of social media can be very different, and we need to be careful in making generalizations about whether it has a “positive” or “negative” impact on friendships

### Potential reliance on SNS as way of avoiding in-person interaction

- **Due to the immediate feedback it provides, social media can potentially be an easier way for some adolescents to maintain interpersonal relationships. However, this also means that adolescents with lower levels of reflective functioning** (RF–defined as “the capacity to understand and interpret one’s own and others’ behaviour as an expression of mental states”) **may be susceptible to over-reliance on social media as a substitute for in-person relationships**[^11]. **This could lead to negative impacts on identity processes if not addressed.**  
- According to one study conducted in Italy, adolescents with deficiencies in RF tend to use SNS more problematically (measured according to the Internet Addiction Test)[^11]  
    - Furthermore, the amount of hours adolescents spent online was positively correlated with problematic social media use[^11]  
- Problematic social media use has been negatively related with identity in-depth exploration and positively related with reconsideration of commitment, adding to evidence that online interactions may not work as a substitute for in-person ones[^11] 

### Strategies for incorporating a healthy mix of in-person and online interpersonal relationships into socialization

- *Schedule meetups, walks, or shared activities with friends in advance – pre-commit*[^12]  
    - Social media may feel easier to prioritize as a means of communication due to its low barrier for entry (can communicate from anywhere). Committing to time spent in-person for friendships/relationships ahead of time provides a way to balance this, so communication takes place both online and offline  
- *Schedule phone and/or video calls with people close to you*, if you cannot meet up regularly offline[^12]   
    - The body language and tone through a call may help balance text/emoji/’like’-only communication that is otherwise common on social media  
- From time to time, consider *participating in shared activities in your community, such as service groups or hobby clubs, that feel meaningful* to you  
    - Can help diversify social circles due to the large variety of people attending community events. This could, in part, counteract the potential for echo chambers on social media[^12]

# FoMO & Procrastination

### How social media has changed social expectations 

- “**Before social media, individuals may have attributed the frequency or lack of** their **interactions with their social group to the constraints of not being physically together**.” **Now**, with social media removing the need for in-person interaction to communicate, **there is a greater focus on the individual making time to respond**[^13]  
    - Can increase pressure of staying on social media to avoid “lost opportunities” of connection: Alutaybi et. al speculated, “the **increased social interaction opportunities and** the **easy access to social media may cause individuals to experience FoMO**”[^13]

### FoMO and its impacts

- **FoMO: “the preoccupation of users of social media with lost opportunities when they are offline or unable or unwilling to** connect and **communicate with others to the extent they wish**”[^13]  
    - Can be further categorized into five primary fears[^13]:   
1) Fear of “missing the ability to be popular … to others”  
2) Fear of “missing the ability to be … interesting to others”  
3) Fear of “not getting the right interpretation of some social interaction”  
4) Fear of “deal\[ing\] with different social networks at one time”  
5) Fear of “missing temporarily available information and timely interactions”  
- Eventual **consequences of FoMO may include**:[^59] [^60]   
    - **Lack of sleep** – see [here](#sleep) for more sleep-related social media impacts  
    - **Reduced competency in daily activities**  
    - **Emotional tension**  
    - **Lack of emotional control** and/or regulation  
    - **Effects on physical well-being**  
    - **Anxiety**  
- FoMO has a positive association with compulsive social media use (CSMU), supported by multiple studies[^14]  
    - Suggests relationship between experiencing FoMO and some compulsive behaviors  
    - CSMU has been linked to sleep impacts, explaining one key eventual impact of FoMO[^14]

### Using social media as means of escape/procrastination

- Procrastination: “a voluntary delay of activities which often leads people to postpone or avoid working on their original tasks”[^15]  
    - Also known as “self-regulation failure”[^15]  
    - Academic procrastination “is consistently associated with reduced student achievement” in past scientific literature, meaning that any potential association between social media (over)use and academic procrastination should be closely monitored for students[^16]  
- **SNS can be an avoidance/escape mechanism for users with lower levels of emotional self-regulation**[^15]  
- **One study of** 450 **high school students** in Turkey **found** that **FoMO has a statistically significant yet weak positive link to academic procrastination levels**–approximately 5% common variance[^16]  
    - This correlation shows a potential intersection between social media-induced fear of missing out and potentially stressful and/or negative impact on academic performance in this population  
    - However, also **important to interpret any impacts of FoMO on academic behavior in the context of individuals’ self-regulation as well as any external influences** (parenting, friends, economic status, demographics, etc.) that may be influencing their experience[^16]  
- In general, it is important to note that some research indicates **high school students tend to have relatively low propensity for FoMO related to SMU to begin with**[^16]  
    - Means that **potential relationship between FoMO and academic procrastination variable applies meaningfully to individuals only insofar as they experience FoMO**–this may be a relatively small proportion of students  
- **Whether general use of social media by students correlates with academic procrastination levels** (outside of individual-specific factors like FoMO) **is still debated by** various **scientific literature**  
    - **One study** conducting surveys **of Grade 11 students at an Indonesian private college** in 2022-2023 **found** that, while there were moderate levels of virtual tolerance, virtual information, virtual communication, and virtual problems among the students – variables gauged by the standardized Social Media Addiction Scale – as well as moderate levels of academic procrastination based on the Academic Procrastination Scale, **there was “no significant relationship between social media addiction and academic procrastination among Grade 11 students”** in the cohort[^17]  
        - The authors **concluded that social media “plays a significant role in students’ lives” given the observance of social media addiction among some** **students, but also that its impact was not “overwhelmingly detrimental”**[^17]. At the same time, the authors **attributed the students’ moderate levels of academic procrastination to other factors besides social media** (time management skills, fear of failure, etc.) given the lack of statistically significant correlation between social media addiction and academic procrastination variables[^17]   
    - **Another study**, conducted through surveys using the Social Networking Time Use Scale (SONTUS) and Academic Procrastination Scale (APS) **at Peshawar University in Pakistan** in 2022, **did find a statistically significant positive correlation (r=.424) between respondents’ social networking time use scores and academic procrastination scores**[^18]  
        - The researchers **also found an inverse relationship between the social networking time use scores/academic procrastination scores, and educational level/academic scores**[^18]  
            - Potentially indicates negative impact of excessive social media time usage on overall student academic performance, as a result of academic procrastination  
        - In general, the **authors concluded students who “excessively” used social media services “are at a greater risk of indulging in academic procrastination”**[^18]  
            - A similar correlational study was conducted at Semarang State University in Indonesia in 2018\. It used the Social Media Usage Intensity Scale to measure the quantity of SMU among students, and again the standardized Academic Procrastination Scale[^19].  
                - It **found that there was a statistically significant correlation between procrastination and social media usage among students at the university**, using Pearson product-moment correlation[^19]  
                    - However, **as with the previous studies it is important to note that correlation does not necessarily mean causation**; while it was true that the higher an individual’s social media use was, the higher their academic procrastination level tended to be, **there are many factors that can mediate relationships w/ procrastination and academic performance and it is possible the association does not mean much in reality**  
- **Both of the studies finding a correlational relationship** between social media use and academic procrastination **among college-age students found their cohorts to have a moderate average and median level of social media use** overall ([Impact of Social Media Usage on Academic Procrastination](impact-of-smu-academic-procrastination.md), [Social Media User Students’ Academic Procrastination](smu-students-academic-procrastination.md)). Therefore, **any potential relationship between social media use and academic procrastination may be impactful primarily for the relatively smaller proportion of students with higher levels of social media use**

### Features of social media that can exacerbate procrastination

- Infinite scrolling, push notifications, variable rewards (i.e. “likes” as an indicator), immersive design, etc.[^15] 

### Countermeasures against FoMO & procrastination

- Potential strategies to avoid FoMO[^13]:  
    - *Set status to show availability*  
    - *Communicate directly w/ contacts about availability and/or maximum time for conversations, in advance*  
    - *Practice self-talk*, e.g.:  
        - “I do not expect interaction from others when I post on social media”   
        - “I am not the only one who does not receive the interaction they expect”  
        - “I do not need to check each notification immediately”  
        - “I do not need to know everything about my contacts”  
        - “I do not need to spend a long time on conversations because I have tasks to do; I have to sleep early, etc.”  
        - My popularity does not depend on an immediate response to the message”  
    - *Engage in offline activities – can clean/organize house, talk to people in-person, take a walk, etc.*  
    - *Create priority list of contacts that are especially important/meaningful to you*  
        - *Mute notifications, except high-importance*  
    - *Physically distance yourself from devices from time to time, and engage in activities you enjoy*  
- For self-help packet summarizing FoMO-R method, developed experimentally by researchers to help combat social media on various major platforms, see [here](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/17/17/6128/s1) (download link)[^61]  
    - Core tools of method:  
1) *Self talk: use “do not” when resisting temptation*  
- “I don’t do X”  
2) *Expectation management: use cognitive reappraisal method*  
- Post on social media w/o expecting interaction from others, clarify to others that may not always reciprocate interaction immediately, and make clear current situation to contacts (e.g. if studying for an exam and unable to check messages)  
3) *Enhance self-esteem*  
- Use positive self talk; see potential self-talk strategies for FoMO in section above  
- Potential strategies to discourage procrastination[^15]:  
    - *Set up autoreply which conveys expectation for later response time*  
    - *Set usage reminders on apps (using tools like Google Digital Wellbeing) and/or time restrictions (by total time on app, or time of day)*  
    - *Set boundaries with friends on availability to respond, in advance*

# Neuroscience on Social Media

### Longitudinal Brain Development and Social Media: Habitual Checking & Excessive Use

- [Association of Habitual Checking Behaviors on Social Media With Longitudinal Functional Brain Development](association-of-habitual-checking-behaviors.md) (2023), a 3-year longitudinal cohort study of fMRI among sixth- and seventh-grade students from 3 public middle schools in rural North Carolina found that **kids who habitually check social media had changes in parts of the brain that control social rewards and punishments**   
    - At age 12, habitual checkers initially showed lower neural sensitivity (hypoactivation) to anticipating social feedback. However, over the 3-year study, their brain activation in these regions significantly increased  
    - Nonhabitual checkers showed the opposite pattern. They started with higher neural sensitivity (hyperactivation) at age 12, which decreased over time  
- Specific brain regions altered by checking behaviors:  
    - Amygdala and posterior insula (regions associated with affective salience, or detecting emotionally important stimuli)  
    - Ventral striatum (region involved in motivational relevance and reward processing)  
    - Dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (region associated with cognitive control and regulation)  
        - Decrease in DLPFC activation may indicate that teens require less effort to control impulsive behaviors as they mature  
        - Increasing activation seen in habitual checkers suggests they become increasingly sensitive to social feedback anticipation over time and have to exert more effort to maintain cognitive control when anticipating social rewards  
- “These platforms give adolescents the opportunity for **increased social interaction at a time when their brains are especially sensitive to social feedback, particularly reward feedback**. Over the course of three years, the researchers looked at the brain activity of teens who habitually checked social media against those who didn’t and found changes in the areas of the brain associated with social rewards and punishments. It’s **too early to say if social media was the only cause of these changes, but it does suggest that more studies are needed** to better understand brain development in the age of social media.”   
  – Dr. Heidi Allison Bender (neuropsychologist w/ NewYork-Presbyterian/Weill Cornell   
- Social media usage weakly associated with a decrease in cerebellum volumes[^20]  
- A 2024 study found that **“children who spent a longer time on social media usage …had a decrease in cerebellum volume”**[^21]  
    - **Initial social media use** at the start of the study **was not associated with changes** in cerebellum volumes; **decrease in cerebellum size was associated specifically with increases in social media use over the 4-year period**  
        - “Excluding time spent on video chatting/texting from social media usage” did not change the direction of observed effects between social media use and cerebellum size over time  
- [Excess social media use in normal populations is associated with amygdala-striatal but not with prefrontal morphology](excess-smu-in-normal-pop-brain-morphology.md)   
- A 2021 study concluded that **there is a “strong and focal relationship between dopamine synthesis capacity in the bilateral posterior putamen and smartphone social app use**, which is also **specific to social app use**.”[^22] 

### Functional Connectivity Changes in Brains of Adolescents With Internet Addiction

- Internet addiction (IA) does not just affect one area of the brain; it affects multiple brain networks (changes often similar to those seen in substance abuse and gambling disorders)[^23]   
- Four primary networks in the adolescent brain significantly impacted by IA:  
    - Executive Control Network (ECN): involved in decision-making, attention, and impulse control  
        - Notable decrease in functional connectivity within the ECN, particularly in the striatum, frontal gyrus, and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex  
            - Leads to “loss of control” over internet usage, increased impulsivity, & reduced ability to display goal-directed behavior  
    - Reward Network  
        - Increased functional connectivity & activation in reward pathway (including nucleus accumbens, caudate, and thalamus)  
            - Creates hypersensitivity to rewards. Similar to drug addiction, electronic gaming (or potentially refreshing feed) promotes striatal dopamine release, stimulating the reward system & overwhelming the brain’s inhibitory controls   
    - Default Mode Network (DMN): involved in self-referential processing and attention  
        - Effects are complex, showing a mix of increases & decreases in connectivity. For instance, connectivity in the posterior cingulate cortex is often altered, which affects attentional processes  
            - Disruptions here are linked to problems with self-monitoring and regulating impulsivity. The severity of IA often correlates with these connectivity changes  
    - Salience Network (SN): helps evaluate internal & external stimuli (detecting what is “important”)  
        - Studies show decreased structural connectivity and integrity in the SN  
            - Adolescents may struggle to effectively inhibit DMN activity or manage desires to use the internet, leading to increased “salience” (or craving) toward internet-related cues

- Adolescent brain is particularly vulnerable to these influences because of **high plasticity** (allowing for increased learning and adaptation but also increasing the risk of addiction), **emotional hyperactivation** (higher sensation-seeking and risk-taking behaviors), and **social reorientation** (heightened sensitivity to social cues and peer relations)   
- Behavioral outcomes of IA may include **impulsivity** (directly resulting from suppressed connection between dorsal striatum & prefrontal cortex), **conflict & withdrawal** (may include mood swings, conflict, or even physical health issues like sleep disruption), **cue reactivity** (altered brain networks make adolescents highly reactive to triggers/cues associated with internet use, making it difficult to avoid usage)

### Neurological Impacts of the Like Button 

- Social media “like” button could be thought of as a form of “quantifiable social endorsement” that activates specific neural circuitry associated with reward, social cognition, & cognitive control  
- Impacts of Receiving Likes and Viewing Popularity:[^9]   
    - Activation of reward circuitry   
        - Nucleus accumbens highly implicated in social media interaction (specifically when receiving positive feedback, aka many likes, one one’s own photos)  
    - Study found that adolescents were significantly more likely to “like” a photo if it already received many likes from peers (heightened conformity)  
    - When teens viewed photos with many likes (versus few), they showed greater activations in neural regions associated with social cognition, imitation, and attention. The visual cortex was more active for popular photos (this may suggest scanning popular images with greater care)  
    - When adolescents viewed “risky” photos (depicting alcohol, smoking, etc.), activation in the cognitive-control network (including the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex and prefrontal cortex) decreased  
        - May suggest that peer influence on social media may “disinhibit cognitive control in high-risk scenarios,” possibly making it more likely for teens to engage in these behaviors  
- The Impact of Giving Likes to Others:[^24]  
    - Study found that the act of providing likes to others activates the same reward circuitry (specifically the striatum and ventral tegmental area) involved in receiving likes  
    - Providing likes was associated with activation in the “salience network” (including the insula and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex), involved in autonomic processing and interoceptive awareness (usually “gut instinct” to decide to like a photo rather than deep/active engagement) 

### Distinct Developmental Windows of Sensitivity to Social Media in Adolescence

- Analysis from 2022 study suggests that there are “distinct development windows of sensitivity to social media in adolescence”[^25]  
    - Higher estimated social media predicts a decrease in life satisfaction ratings one year later (and vice-versa)  
        - Differences also found between ages these windows occurred based on sex  
        - Still, “much needed progress in understanding how social media use affects well-being could be made” by looking at impact of different developmental stages/processes on social media’s effects   
    - Based on **sociocultural changes and life events common in adolescence**, “it is … plausible that \[this **heightens**\] **adolescents’ sensitivity to the interactive, communicative, and self-portraying nature of social media**”  
    - Study found **the most substantial negative relations were found in adolescence**  
        - “The relations in the raw data differed when comparing younger (10-15 years) and older adolescents (16-21 years … ),” showing that **the effects (and therefore healthy approaches to) social media differ based on whether adolescent users are younger or older**   
        - Amongst “older” (16-21 y.o.) adolescents, “those who estimated they engaged in very low or very high social media use reported lower life satisfaction ratings than those who estimated that they used between ‘less than an hour’ and ‘1-3 h’ of social media a day”   
            - Not observed in younger adolescents (however, younger adolescents “showed more prominent differences between males and females” in SMS impact on life satisfaction)  
        - **For females**, the authors observed a **period of sensitivity from ages 11-13**, “when increases in estimated social media use from expected levels predicted a decrease in life satisfaction ratings from expected levels one year later”   
            - This **may be due to** maturation/**developmental processes** in puberty **that occur earlier for females** than males  
        - **For males,** statistically significant, potential **period of sensitivity** is **from ages 14-15**, with similar social media use prediction of lowered satisfaction ratings  
        - Data showed **later increase in sensitivity for both sexes around age 19**  
            - This “**might be related to changes in the social environment such as a move away from home** and subsequent disruptions in social networks”

# Sleep

### Screen Time and Sleep

- **Adolescents who frequently use social media during the day report less total sleep time**[^62] and **poorer sleep quality**[^63]   
- International research data shows that 4-18% of 15 y/os from 42 countries/regions reported high levels of problematic SMU in 2017-2018[^64] (Problematic social media use: “loss of control over one’s social media use, accompanied by significant impairments in daily-life functioning”[^26])  
- Frequency of social media use (especially in the evenings) is related to a delay in bedtime[^65], increased difficulty falling asleep[^66] [^67], shorter sleep duration[^66] [^68], and poorer sleep quality[^63] [^69]   
- Exposure to too much evening light (e.g., blue light from screens) can affect the brain’s clock and the production of the sleep hormone melatonin \-\> disrupts sleep time & quality  
- “Sleep in teenagers can be improved by just one week of limiting their evening exposure to light-emitting screens on phones, tablets and computers, according to findings to be presented in Lyon, at the European Society of Endocrinology annual meeting, ECE 2019.”[^27]   
    - Study indicates that by simply limiting exposure to blue-light emitting devices in the evening, in one week, adolescents can:  
        - Improve sleep quality  
        - Reduce symptoms of fatigue, lack of concentration and bad mood  
- Exposure to too much evening light (e.g., blue light from screens) can affect the brain’s clock and the production of the sleep hormone melatonin \-\> disrupts sleep time & quality  
    - Those who had 4+ hours per day of screen time had, on average, 30 minutes later sleep onset and wake up times than those who recorded less than 1 hour per day of screen time, as well as more symptoms of sleep loss  
- Online activities (e.g., chatting) right before bedtime may cause “emotional, mental, and/or physiological arousal, which may, in turn, lead to more difficulty falling asleep and poorer sleep quality”[^26]   
- Because social media does not include a defined endpoint (i.e., infinite scroll), adolescents may find it challenging to stop[^26] 

### Social Media’s Long and Short-Term Effects on Sleep Quality 

- National Sleep Foundation (NSF) recommends that children ages 6-13 get 9-11 hours of sleep per night, and adolescents 14-17 years of age get 8-10 hours per night[^70]  
- Risks associated with not getting sufficient sleep for children & adolescents:  
    - Trouble thinking clearly  
    - Poor academic performance[^68]   
    - Feeling less awake or aware  
    - Depressed mood  
    - Anxiety symptoms  
    - Obesity  
- Lack of sleep can cause tiredness & poor concentration short-term[^27]   
    - Long-term, it can increase risk of chronic health issues such as obesity, diabetes, and heart disease  
    - Other studies have suggested that sleep deprivation related to screen time may affect children & adolescents more than adults  
- “Psychological well-being influences CSMU (compulsive social media use), which in concurrence with sleep habits, influences the association between FoMO and problematic sleep”[^14]  
    - Study found positive association between CMSU with problematic sleep due to social media to be significant among students → suggests social media engagement takes precedence over sleep quality or duration for students  
    - Study found positive association with FoMO (fear of missing out) and poor social media sleep hygiene to be significant for students, which is supported by prior research on effects of FoMO on young social media users (e.g.[^71] [^72]) → suggests that “students give considerable value to FoMO and may consequently experience higher levels of nomophobia”  
- “Strict parental rules about internet and smartphone use before sleep might prevent negative consequences of social media use on bedtime and sleep quality, but only among less engaged social media users. Once adolescents are highly engaged social media users, strict parental rules do not seem to prevent negative media influences on sleep.”[^26]  
    - “...Parental rules regarding social media use in the hour before going to sleep generated more convincing results than parental rules regarding taking a smartphone or tablet to the bedroom; therefore, they may be more effective in preventing sleep problems”  
- B-SATED (Behavior, Satisfaction, Alertness, Timing, Efficiency, and Duration)[^28] = comprehensive model designed to optimize sleep health for youth adolescents by addressing six dimensions[^73]   
    - Sleep-related **behaviors** include factors that promote sleep (e.g., consistent sleep schedule, bedtime routine) & those that could **negatively impact sleep (e.g., caffeine and technology use in the hour before bed)**  
    - **Satisfaction** with sleep is a subjective feeling of whether the person thought their sleep was good/bad in quality. Phones and other electronic devices can play a role in this **(if phones are keeping people awake and distracting them, they may report less satisfaction with sleep quality)**  
    - **Alertness** means the ability to remain alert and awake during the day. **Cross-sectional study of 4153 U.S. adolescents ages 12-17 years found that adolescents who posted on or checked social media more frequently and those who perceived social media to be more important for social belonging had higher levels of daytime sleepiness**[^74]   
    - **Timing** of sleep refers to when a person goes to bed and wakes up with a regular routine aligning with natural sleep cycles showing best results. A **review article found several studies that suggested adolescents may postpone bedtime so they can continue using devices for entertainment (behavioral bedtime delay).** When adolescents postpone sleep, they may not be able to also postpone wake-up time, leading to shorter sleep[^75]   
    - Sleep **efficiency** refers to how easy it is for one to fall asleep & return to sleep if they wake in the middle of the night. **Phone alerts may interfere with sleep efficiency**  
    - Sleep duration refers to the total amount of sleep a person gets per day. Number of recommended hours an individual should get changes across development  
- Tips for promoting healthy adolescent sleep habits:[^28]  
    - Consider *putting phone on charger somewhere where you can’t reach from bed* to avoid waking up throughout night to check phone  
        - Can also use phone settings to *silence notifications at night*  
    - Work to *build bedtime routine that doesn’t include social media in hours leading up to bedtime*  
        - E.g., showering, choosing outfit for next day, reading, journaling, listening to podcast, doing art  
        - Build in time for social media earlier in evening  
    - *Participate in physical activity along with healthy eating to remain alert and awake during day*  
    - *Aim for recommended amount of sleep each night* based on age & development  
        - Try to keep routine consistent each night to coincide with natural sleep-wake rhythms

# Academic Performance

### Heavy and/or “Problematic” Social Media Use vs. Academic Performance

- **General technology use of greater than 5 hours a day**–often academically categorized as “heavy use”-**\-was found** in a study of over 10,000 students **to associate with lower academic performance**[^29]   
    - Around 25% of students in this study self-reported that they “neglected homework because spending more time on devices” quite often or very often[^29]   
        - **Note that neither of these statistics are social-media specific.**   
        - However, with social media a popular form of online activity, it is still important to investigate the time we spend on social media if it contributes to these overall impacts of high screen time.   
            - This is particularly relevant if we consider the **hypothesis that high levels of online use are associated with negative impacts due to their displacement of valuable offline activities.**   
- A 2024 study conducted on US middle schoolers aged 11-15 found that their **academic achievement decreased as** their **Facebook, Instagram, Snapchat, and Twitter/X use increased**[^30]. **Note this is a correlation and not a causation relationship, meaning there are many possible explanations.**  
    - This study **focuses exclusively on relatively younger adolescents; this is relevant because impacts may be more pronounced in this developmental window** (see [here](#impact-of-age/maturity,-gender-identity,-and-parental-intervention) and [here](#distinct-developmental-windows-of-sensitivity-to-social-media-in-adolescence) for more information). It is **also important to investigate this age range of adolescents because it is the fastest growing population of social media users**[^30]  
    - UD Associate Professor Mellissa Gordon, a co-author of the study, provided a couple hypotheses for the correlation between increased social media use and decreased grades: “**Attention \[students\] would typically invest in their schoolwork is diverted to social media use**, which ultimately affects their ability to perform well in school. But lower academic achievement may also result from other aspects of development that are affected by social media use. For example, **social media use can disrupt healthy family functioning or peer relationships, which can then lower early adolescents’ performance in school**”[^30]  
        - Note these are all speculative hypotheses, and cannot be evaluated holistically for accuracy  
- In a sample of around 10,000 adolescents aged 14-17, “**adolescents who engaged heavily in SNS scored significantly lower in offline Activities and Academic performance compared with adolescents who used SNS moderately**”[^31]  
    - Important takeaway: moderate use may not be very harmful, but excessive use perhaps can be

### Impact of age/maturity, gender identity, and parental intervention

- In a survey on the association between general technology use and academic performance, it was found that female-identifying individuals were more likely to be affected by time spent online than male-identifying individuals among the respondents[^29]   
    - This is an important difference to investigate further in future studies. Currently, there is not much evidence/hypotheses explaining this phenomena, or its accuracy.  
- **The impact of heavy social media use on academic performance may be more pronounced for younger individuals**, suggesting that differences in developmental stages and/or life experience play a role in moderating this relationship  
    - In a study from 2014 establishing an association between “heavy” (\>=2) social media use and relatively lower academic performance scores, “differences between moderate and heavier users \[were\] … larger for the younger age group” and “the negative effect of heavier use was more pronounced in the younger age group”[^31]  
        - The same study also found that “social competence scores appeared not to differ” between moderate and heavy social networking service users. In fact, **older adolescents** (defined as age 16+) **in the “heavy” use category exhibited slightly higher scores in social competence** metrics **compared to those in the “moderate” use category**.  
            - Provides strong argument for different guidelines for social media usage at different ages  
    - *One promising avenue for mitigating any potential negative effects* of social media use on academic performance, particularly for adolescents in the younger age group, *may be establishing consistent parental communication*   
        - The study investigating middle school students’ social media use vs. academic performance found that **parental communication played a significant role in whether school grades increased or decreased**, countering part of the negative correlative relationship between SMU and grades[^30]  
            - Further investigation is needed to determine whether this moderated the relationship is due to inherent impacts of adolescent-parent interaction, or simply because extensive parental communication often involved limits/expectations being more strictly imposed on social media use.   
                - Either way, it is worth investigating what actions parents can take to aid young adolescents in navigating healthy social media use. For reference on potential actions, see [here](#info-for-parents-&-educators).

# Body Image/Eating Disorders

### Social Media’s Impact on Body Image and Eating Disorders

- “After substance abuse, eating disorders rank second in the mortality rate of any mental health issue”[^32]  
- SMU was connected with body image problems and disordered eating, especially among young women[^33]  
- Constant favorable feeds of friends can influence certain young individuals, leading them to make unfavorable comparisons between themselves and others (this can apply directly to unrealistic body image & filters)  
- On social media, adolescents may frequently engage in trolling (which can focus on body shaming, individual abilities, language, lifestyle, etc.) for amusement without recognizing the potentially harmful consequences  
- A study found that 41.7% of its participants (and just under 60% of female participants) were found via EAT-26 to **have a likely chance of developing eating disorders**. Similarly **42% overall** and 57.8% of female participants **were labeled as having social media addiction**[^32]  
    - Associating general results for participants, **social media had a weak positive relationship with overall eating disorders,** particularly with **bulimia and food preoccupation**  
- In another study, **participants that were exposed to weight loss content or body positivity/neutrality content on social media reported “significantly higher BMI, lower body appreciation, greater fears of negative appearance evaluation, and more frequent binge eating, and laxative use” than those who were not**[^34]  
    - Indicates that specific types of content on social media play a major role in impact on body image. Supports idea that social media should be used intentionally to avoid potential negative effects  
- **Amount of social media use** each day **was** “**significantly and inversely associated with BAS scores** (measures an individual's sensitivity to rewards and motivation to pursue goals) **and significantly and positively correlated with BMI, ratings on the FNAES, and frequency of binge eating and vomiting**”  
    - Breaking this down, it was **specifically exposure to weight-loss content on social media** which **affected BAS and FNAES scores and frequency of binge eating** rather than just time spent on social media overall.  
    - Similarly, “**respondents spending more time on social media** … **only reported high binge frequency if they were also exposed to weight loss content**”   
    - Therefore, while social media use is clearly associated with negative body image effects, it is also the weight loss content presented to users/seeked out by users on SM which is responsible for this association–not the social media itself  
- Interestingly, the analysis found that consuming **body-positivity or neutral content** on social media **did not benefit or harm participants’ body image perception and eating disorder behaviors**  
- Overall conclusion of the study: “**time spent on social media and number of platforms accessed were generally unrelated to outcomes of interest**,” but “**there were significant** main **effects of viewing weight loss content on social media on** indices of **body image satisfaction and disordered eating behaviors**, with exposure to weight loss content associated with lower body appreciation, greater fears of negative appearance evaluation, and more frequent binge eating”  
- In a 2024 survey, **17% of highest social media users expressed poor body image** compared with 6% of the lowest users[^3]  
- Review of 50 studies across 17 countries between 2016 and 2021 suggested that relentless online exposure to largely unattainable physical ideals may trigger a distorted sense of self and eating disorders (particularly among girls)[^35]   
- Potential Solution: based on research, authors concluded that “**interventions targeting negative consequences of social media use should focus on addressing content consumed**, rather than time spent on social media platforms”[^34]

# Behavioral Impacts (Including Stress & Depression)

### Social Media Red Flags: When Does Use Become Problematic?

- Social media use may get to the point where it raises concerns including time adolescents spend on platforms, the type(s) of content they consume/are exposed to, and the “degree” of essential activity disruption due to social media (e.g., sleep, physical activity)[^36]  
    - Social media may also affect young users in “different ways, depending on their individual strengths and vulnerabilities, and based on cultural, historical, and socio-economic factors”  
    - According to the U.S. Surgeon General’s 2024 report, “frequent social media use may be associated with distinct changes in the developing brain,” potentially affecting things like emotional learning, behavior, impulse control, and emotional regulation

### Time Spent on Social Media: Behavioral Impacts

- A 2024 survey revealed that U.S. teens average 4.8 hours on seven popular social media apps (including YouTube, Instagram, and TikTok, all of which accounted for 87% of their social media time)  
    - Survey also found that **41% of teens with the highest social media use rated their overall mental health as poor or very poor**, compared with 23% of those with the lowest use[^3]  
        - **10% of the highest use group** expressed **suicidal intent or self-harm** in the past 12 months compared with 5% of the lowest use group  
- Social media may have positive effects on connectivity, idea sharing, and online learning. Being socially linked with others helped alleviate stress, worry, and melancholy[^33]  
    - Mental health, health behavior, physical health, & mortality risk are all affected by the quantity/quality of social contacts  
- A systematic review identified 11 studies that examined connection between social media use and depression symptoms in children & adolescents[^33]  
    - **Research demonstrated modest but statistically significant association between social media use and depression symptoms** (however, relationship’s causality is unclear – more studies needed)   
- Additional research studied effects of technology on psychosocial functioning, health, and well-being of adolescents and young adults  
    - **“Higher levels of social media usage were connected with worse mental health outcomes and associated with an increased risk of internalizing and externalizing difficulties among adolescents, especially females”**  
    - **“Use of social media was also connected with body image problems and disordered eating, especially among young women, and social media may be a risk factor for alcohol consumption and associated consequences among adolescents and young adults”**  
- **“Cyberbullying victimization is connected with poorer mental health outcomes in teenagers, including an increased risk of sadness and anxiety”**[^37]   
- **“The use of social media was also connected with more depressive symptoms and excessive reassurance-seeking, but also with greater popularity and perceived social support**[^38]**, as well as appearance comparisons and body image worries, especially among young women”**[^39]   
- **“Children and adolescents’ bedtime media device use was substantially related to inadequate sleep quantity, poor sleep quality, and excessive daytime drowsiness”**[^40]   
- “While online friends can be a significant source of social support, **in-person social support appears to provide greater protection against persecution”**[^41]   
- According to research study of American teens aged 12-15, those who used social media 3+ hours a day faced twice the risk of having negative mental health outcomes (including depression and anxiety symptoms)[^42]   
- The impacts of limiting time spent on social media may vary depending on average use. A study found that **college students with limited social media use that were restricted to 30 minutes of social media a day had less depression symptoms than the control group. However, students limited to 30 minutes of social media a day that described themselves as highly active on social media reported more loneliness, anxiety, and low self-esteem.**[^43]  
- Recommendations for Setting Time Limits:[^43]  
    - Be easy on yourself   
        - Acknowledge managing social media is challenging and takes time & practice  
    - Shift focus to how and why you use social media  
        - Instead of tracking time, it may be effective to think about when, where, how, and why you use social media  
        - For instance, think about avoiding social media at bedtime, which has been shown to disrupt sleep. Instead build in time during the day for important activities on social media that can prevent you from using it at night  
    - Be able to explain why and how you use social media  
    - Develop a plan w/ parents/caregivers  
    - It’s (more than) okay to ask for help  
        - If social media is having a negative effect on your life, *please* reach out to trusted adults for help\!  
        - If you feel comfortable, talk to your parents/guardians about social media & what you’re struggling with  
            - E.g., ask them to talk about how they manage their use or what they do when they run into a tricky situation. They might help you think critically about what to do & how to protect yourself and your well-being  
- Study surveying U.S. adolescents found that **new media screen activities** (**specifically electronic device use and social media**, rather than any screens like TVs) **had a higher correlation in their relationship to mental health in girls than boys**[^44]  
    - “**Social media use was significantly correlated with depressive symptoms among girls … but not among boys** … (with demographic controls)”   
    - Overall, “**adolescents using social media sites every day were 13% more likely to report high levels of depressive symptoms than those using social media less often**”   
    - Additionally, results suggested that in-person interaction might have an offsetting effect on negative SMU effects: “**among those low in in-person social interaction, social media use had a significant effect on depressive symptoms … but among those high in in-person social interaction, social media did not have a significant effect**”   
- One longitudinal study on adolescents found “n**o evidence that time spent on social media or gaming frequency predicted later internalizing symptoms among girls or boys. Sensitivity analyses that distinguished active vs. passive social media use replicated findings**” ()  
    - Speculative commentary:  
        - A lot of possible colliding factors for generational behavioral changes BESIDES SMU/screen time: “could be social influences from other children, more parents working outside of home, TV shows, music, drinking, school pressure, lack of being able to be w/ a lot of generations of family, lack of affordability of childcare, etc.”   
        - Stress from constant social media notifications could be associated but not predictable  
            - Getting distracted in class from time-to-time by notifications does not necessarily lead to pitfalls, behavioral issues, or intellectual defects   
        - “More than just time spent on social media, content which children consume seems to matter as well, as well as timing of use (e.g., is it interfering with sleep? Are they exposed to content in the news or online victimization that may be contributing to some stressors?)”

### Social Media Addiction / Social Media Fatigue

- Study conducted via surveying young adults in Spain found that **“addiction to Instagram was mainly due to the fact that the respondents spent more time on Instagram than initially intended, which results in their incapability of reducing its usage”**[^45]  
    - At the same time, “the risk of losing a significant relationship, job or career opportunity was not found to be a significant consequence of their addictive Instagram usage”   
        - Suggests that, while Instagram addiction may have negative correlates beyond just taking up time of users–such as emotional fatigue and instastress–it is not as detrimental as other addictions in its interference in users’ daily lives   
- Overall (to the end of their research question), authors concluded that **“addiction partially mediates the impact of overuse on emotional fatigue and instastress”**  
    - Essentially, this means that overuse can often lead to addiction, which then leads to negative consequences of Instagram use. However, the only partial mediation shows that simply overuse, without a user necessarily reaching the threshold of addiction, correlates directly to negative consequences as well  
- Study (online questionnaire for social media users – note: not specifically adolescents or young adults) found that “while enjoying the benefits of online communication, many young individuals are experiencing various challenges such as negative comparisons, too much information, and difficulties interacting with others”[^46]  
    - **Social media self-efficacy was found to have a significant negative impact on social media fatigue**  
    - **Compulsive social media use and FoMO had significant positive impacts on social media fatigue**  
    - **Information overload has a significant positive impact on social media fatigue**  
        - **However, the chronic fear and stress of not having the same experience of others once withdrawing from social media can also lead to fatigue**  
    - **Social media helpfulness had no impact on social media fatigue**  
    - **Social comparison had no significant impact on social media fatigue**  
    - **Impact of privacy concerns on social media fatigue was not significant**  
    - **Social media fatigue had a positive and significant impact on social anxiety**  
    - **Furthermore, FoMO mediated the anxiety-social media fatigue association across dimensions**  
    - **Social media fatigue was found to significantly impact lurking**

### Consuming News on Social Media

- Social media has made it possible to have access to the latest news at any given moment, in real-time, right at your fingertips[^47]  
    - Most teens report preferring to get their news from social media and/or YouTube than traditional news sources  
    - Teens who get news from social media & YouTube are also much more likely to consume news from influencers and content creators who may not be reliable sources   
    - On the flip side, consuming news on social media might increase teens’ likelihood of participating in civic events & volunteering within community  
- Some things to do to stay connected while protecting mental well-being:[^47]  
    - Limiting the types of quantity of content you see  
        - Some resources for adjusting settings:   
            - Managing content controls – TikTok[^48]   
            - Navigating tragic events (which can include distressing news coverage & social media content) – TikTok[^49]   
            - Adjusting sensitive content controls – Instagram[^50]   
            - How to stop seeing suggested posts with certain words or phrases – Instagram[^51]   
            - How to manage and adjust recommended content – YouTube[^52]   
    - Go directly to credible sources  
        - Some resources for media literacy and identifying credible sources:  
            - Is This Legit: A Media Literacy Skills Recap[^53]   
            - Echo Chambers: How They’re Created and How to Avoid Them[^54]   
            - Tips for Detecting Doctored or Out of Context Videos[^55] 

### Identity Development on Social Media

- Studies found mixed results on the relationship between time spent on social media and identity exploration outcomes, but did reliably conclude that “**social media use seems to be important for identity exploration among those who actively participate**”[^56]  
    - Suggests that the **type of social media used may be a more important factor in** regulating the **relationship between social media and identity**, **than** the **amount of social media use**   
- A couple studies found that “ability and opinion comparison” activities on social media “may be associated with identity confusion, which is also beneficial for engaging in further exploration and making new commitments”   
- Many studies found very **mixed results on “the relationship between time spent on social media and self-concept clarity”**  
- Based on studies comparing the way adolescents presented themselves online and their self-concept clarity, authors concluded that “when individuals present themselves in a manner that aligns with their authentic selves, it fosters self-concept clarity”  
    - That being said, “it might also be the case that those with clearer self-concept are more confident to be who they actually are on social media”  
- For **identity distress**, **studies were** once again **mixed**, **but** one **potential relationship was ability comparison activities** on social media **correlating to identity distress**  
- Authors’ primary conclusion was that “**it is *not* the amount of time spent on social media that is critical, but rather the activities undertaken during this time that seem to play a role for aspects of identity development**”[^56]  
- For a study involving a survey of high school students in Italy, authors concluded: **“results … showed that problematic social networking sites use (PSNSU) fully mediated the relationship between reflective functioning** (RF – defined as “the capacity to understand and interpret one’s own and others’ behaviour as an expression of mental states such as feelings, thoughts, fantasies, beliefs, and desires” (Fonagy et al., 2018)) **and identity in-depth exploration, and it partially mediated the relationship between RF and reconsideration of commitment”**[^11]  
    - “**Adolescents with deficiencies in RF tended to use social networking sites more problematically**, and, **in turn, PSNSU was negatively related with identity in-depth exploration and positively related with reconsideration of commitment**”   
        - Suggests that **adolescents finding difficulty building in-person relationships may turn to social media, but heavy reliance on social media use leads to difficulty** in **exploring & committing to well-developed personal and social identity**  
            - Potentially due to immediate and (sometimes) negative comparisons abundant in social media interactions 

# Info for Parents & Educators

### Setting limits on social media

- Mixed findings about effectiveness of limiting social media use; **setting limits is beneficial for some, could be “detrimental” to others depending how to use it**[^57]   
- “Knowing that, try to create a culture at home where all phones are turned off by a certain time, and make sure it’s at least one hour before going to bed” –Dr. Poncin[^36]

### Tips on reducing social media use

- [Tips](tips-to-manage-sm-use-for-teens.md) on reducing social media use:  
    - Set focus times, switch on “do-not-disturb” or “focus mode” during times such as designated homework time, school, or bedtime  
    - Develop schedule including time for offline hobbies/interests  
        - [Non-screen activities for teenagers](non-screen-activities-for-teenagers.md)   
    - Plan regular sleep routine that helps with relaxation  
    - Consider talking to family about establishing Family Media Plan that can provide ideas for rules, guidelines, and shared media experiences  
    - Explore variety of control settings and understand how to curate feed and see beneficial content[^58]

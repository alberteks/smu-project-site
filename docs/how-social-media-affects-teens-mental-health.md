**How Social Media Affects Your Teen’s Mental Health: A Parent’s Guide** (URL: [https://www.yalemedicine.org/news/social-media-teen-mental-health-a-parents-guide](https://www.yalemedicine.org/news/social-media-teen-mental-health-a-parents-guide))    
*(Published January 2024, updated June 2024\)*

* In spring 2023, US surgeon general released advisory in which he cites growing evidence that social media is causing harm to young people’s mental health  
    * In 2024, same guy calls for surgeon general’s warning label on social media platforms   
    * Still, surgeon general suggests more research needed to fully understand social media’s impact  
    * Advice raises variety of concerns, including time adolescents spend on platforms, type of content consumed/exposed to, “degree” of essential activity disruption (e.g., sleep, physical activity)  
        * Also points out that SMU can affect young users in “different ways, depending on their individual strengths and vulnerabilities, and based on cultural, historical, and socio-economic factors”  
* Brain goes through highly sensitive period between ages 10-19, when identities and feelings of self-worth are forming  
    * According to surgeon general report, “frequent social media use may be associated with distinct changes in the developing brain,” potentially affecting things like emotional learning, behavior, impulse control, & emotional regulation  
* Teens experience benefits from social media when they use it to foster positive connections with others who share common interests/identities, creating space for self-expression  
    * May create opportunities for “positive interactions with more diverse peer groups than are available to them offline”  
* 2022 survey of American teens and their parents showed a majority of respondents felt that social media helps teenagers feel more accepted (58%), like they have people who can support them through tough times (67%), that they have a place to show their creative side (71%), and that they are more connected to what’s going on in their friends’ lives (80%)   
    * “\[Posting and messaging on social media is\] no different than 30 years ago when adolescents would be on the phone for three hours connecting with their friends–only now you’re online with your friends, saying, ‘Meet you after school,’ or ‘Did you hear about this?’” (Dr. Poncin)  
* According to research study of American teens aged 12-15, those who used social media 3+ hours a day faced twice the risk of having negative mental health outcomes (including depression and anxiety symptoms)   
    * Advisory states that other studies “point to a higher relative concern of harm in adolescent girls and those already experiencing poor mental health, as well as for particular health outcomes, such as cyberbullying-related depression, body image and disordered eating behaviors, and poor sleep quality linked to social media use.”  
    * “...Social media algorithms are built to promote whatever you seem interested in,” says Dr. Mayes. “If a teen searches for any kind of mental health condition, such as depression or suicide, it’s going to feed them information about those things, so soon they may begin to think that everyone around them is depressed or thinking about suicide, which is not necessarily good for mental health.”  
* Teens can easily access extreme, inappropriate, and harmful content on social media  
    * According to Dr. Murthy’s report, in certain cases, deaths have been linked to suicide- and self-harm-related content  
        * Discussing/showing content around these behaviors can normalize them too  
    * Review of 50 studies across 17 countries between 2016 and 2021 suggested that relentless online exposure to largely unattainable physical ideals may trigger a distorted sense of self and eating disorders (particularly among girls)  
    * People who target adolescents (for instance, “adults seeking to sexually exploit children, to financially extort them through the threat or actual distribution of intimate images”) may use social media platforms for these types of predatory behaviors  
* Excessive SMU can disrupt important healthy behaviors and overstimulate the brain’s reward center and, when the stimulation becomes excessive, can trigger pathways comparable to addiction  
    * Excessive SMU has been linked to sleep problems, attention problems, and feelings of exclusion in adolescents (and sleep is particularly essential for the healthy development of teens)   
* Roughly 90% of teens aged 13 to 17 report using social media, and nearly half claim they are on social media “almost constantly”   
    * Most used platform was YouTube, followed by TikTok, Instagram, and Snapchat  
* Experts still exploring whether there is a “right age” for a child to access social media because adolescents mature at different rates (making it hard to generalize)  
    * Although minimum age most commonly required by social media platforms in the U.S. is 13, “nearly 40% of children ages 8-12 use social media,” according to Dr. Murthy’s report (indicating how difficult it can be to enforce these rules without parental supervision)  
    * Dr. Poncin says, “In my opinion, elementary school-age children should not have full-on internet access using a device with all the social media apps.”   
        * Instead, they can start with a “dumbphone,” a cell phone that doesn’t have email, an internet browser, and other smartphone features  
    * For middle-schoolers who show maturity and responsibility (who can get themselves to sleep and do their homework, for example), Dr. Poncin notes additional access may be fine (but he suggests delaying full access to smartphones for as long as possible)  
* Research shows relationship between SMU and poor sleep quality, reduced sleep duration, and sleep difficulties in young people   
    * For teens, poor sleep is linked to emotional health issues and higher suicide risk  
    * According to Dr. Murthy’s report, on a typical weekday, nearly one-third of adolescents report using screen media until midnight or later  
    * “Knowing that, try to create a culture at home where all phones are turned off by a certain time, and make sure it’s at least one hour before going to bed,” Dr. Poncin says

**What Should Policymakers Do about Social Media and Minors?** (URL: [https://www.thecgo.org/research/what-should-policymakers-do-about-social-media-and-minors/](https://www.thecgo.org/research/what-should-policymakers-do-about-social-media-and-minors/)) 

*(Published Jan 2023\)*

* In his March 2022 State of the Union address, Biden talked about the “harms of social media” on children & called on Congress to ban targeted advertising \+ strengthen privacy protections for children  
* California passes bill that adds lots of new requirements to keep its users under 18 safe  
  * Other legislation is following (\*note: these are as of 2023\)  
    * 3 major bills in Congress advanced that focus on/include portions intended to protect teens on social media  
    * All members states in the EU adopted Digital Services Act, which requires platforms “to take appropriate and proportionate measures to ensure the privacy, safety, and security of children on their services”  
* The *Wall Street Journal’s* publication of “The Facebook Files” in September 2021 prompted congressional hearings & high-profile interviews w/ former Meta employee \-\> brought to light Meta’s internal research on effects their services might have on teens  
* The Concerns about Social Media, Children, and Teens  
  * Lawmakers currently most concerned about  
    * Negative mental health effects  
    * Privacy (mostly targeted advertising)  
    * Illegal content (e.g., CSAM (child sexual abuse material) and drugs)  
  * Policy responses often follow a predictable “panic cycle,” which usually ends in healthy assimilation of new technologies into society  
    * Trusting beginnings  
    * Rising panic  
      * Risk of escalating to hysteria  
    * Deflating fears  
    * Moving on  
  * Nature of first half of cycle can lead to overreaction, which will “likely mean worse problems and certainly stymie future benefits”  
* Studies on Mental Health Effects Do Not Warrant Bans  
  * Early concerns about health and well-being for minors on social media focused mostly on inappropriate content and child predators  
    * In 2008, to protect teens & children from predators, MySpace (wow, this is old) led efforts to study age verification and safety features; here’s an excerpt from the final report:  
      * “Technology can play a helpful role, but there is no one technological solution or specific combination of technological solutions to the problem of online safety for minors. Instead, a combination of technologies, in concert with parental oversight, education, social services, law enforcement, and sound policies by social network sites and service providers may assist in addressing specific problems that minors face online.”  
    * Many additional books, articles, studies, films, & advocacy efforts between 2017 and now together built to a “crescendo,” with comparisons to tobacco and claims of the loss of free will   
  * Despite the headlines and dominant narratives, a conclusive, casual relationship between social media and negative mental health effects for the typical teen user has not been made yet  
    * Linkage “correlational at best”  
    * “Correlational studies consistently show a link between heavy social media use and mood disorders, but the size of the relationship is disputed.” –Social psychologist Jonathan Haidt  
    * Most significant, although still disputed, connection is among females aged 12-15 and 19-21  
    * Inconclusive data on suicide, self-harm, and depression among teens from social media as well  
  * “Teens themselves paint a more nuanced picture of adolescent life on social media”  
  * In famous “Facebook files” published by the Wall Street Journal & discussed by Frances Haugen in media appearances and congressional testimony, the number of teens who said Instagram made them feel better about themselves was almost 2x the number of those who said it made them feel worse  
  * Experiences vary from individual-to-individual   
* The Harms of Data Collection for Teens Are Not Clear  
  * COPPA (Children’s Online Privacy Protection Act) passed by Congress in 1998 since most platforms are designed for users \>13 years old  
  * Not clear that ban on TV advertising for children would be effective or helpful, so they didn’t proceed with some laws to do so around 50 years ago   
    * Base assumptions about advertising to children and its effects are similar – restrictions then and now would likely diminish the welfare of consumers, including teens  
  * Targeted advertising for teens may have benefits, including educational opportunities and mental health resources  
    * Targeted advertising can even promote safety on these platforms because advertising can be vetted as age-appropriate  
      * Ban on teen-targeted ads mean teens will likely see advertisements meant for adults  
* More Resources Are Necessary to Enforce CSAM  
  * Policymakers concerned for the safety of minors online should first focus on combating CSAM  
  * Social media can be a place where CSAM is developed & trafficked, but the problem is much larger than mainstream social media platforms  
* The Trade-offs of Age Verification Requirements   
  * Fundamentally, any sort of public policy-based ban, restriction, or requirements for minors will require platforms to know the ages of those using their platform  
  * Policy organizations and pundits have suggested states or the federal government place restrictions on minors’ access to social media during certain times or fully ban social media use by minors  
    * Banning minors from social media assumes its negative effects outweigh benefits and that any nongovernmental solution is inadequate  
      * Likely not the case, and outright bans have clear downsides  
      * Any teen who circumvents requirements (lies about their age) could be prosecuted as a “lawbreaker”   
      * Current industries like the adult entertainment, gambling, and sports betting industries already have to deal with such legal requirements  
      * Any age verification method necessitates more data will need to be collected by the companies or a third-party firm  
        * Creates more problems in the realms of privacy and cybersecurity  
      * In order to be compliant, age requirements for social media platforms would mean that all users of that platform will be subject to more data collection  
  * Forcing platforms to comply w/ new age requirements before an effective age verification solution is found is likely to result in more data collection of teens or in teens losing access to platforms they use and enjoy  
* What Should Policymakers Do?  
  * Allocate more funding to law enforcement to combat CSAM at the federal, state, and local levels  
    * Provide more resources and training for officers at the federal, state, and local levels who handle CSAM cases  
    * Sufficient mental health support needs to be made available for officers at all levels working on cases  
    * Companies should be given more time to report instances of detected CSAM  
  * Policymakers should empower those closest to the teens who need help, be they parents, caregivers, educators, or teens themselves  
  * Policymakers, the media, and the public should hold companies to a high standard and join to call attention to the resources the large platforms do and should provide for parents and caregivers  
  * Given the inconclusive nature of social media’s impact on the mental health of kids and teens, more studies should be encouraged and perhaps funded by the government  
  * Policymakers should not vilify companies that actively study these issues and seek to develop safe apps and services for teens

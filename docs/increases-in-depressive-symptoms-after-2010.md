**Increases in Depressive Symptoms, Suicide-Related Outcomes, and Suicide Rates Among U.S. Adolescents After 2010 and Links to Increased New Media Screen Time** (URL: [https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/cs565/sp2018/Live1\_Depression\&ScreenTime.pdf](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/cs565/sp2018/Live1_Depression&ScreenTime.pdf), access needed [https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2167702617723376](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2167702617723376)) 

*(Published Nov 2017\)*

* Study aims to examine mental health trends in adolescents (whether mental health issues like suicidal ideation have increased over time, for example) and use correlational analysis to determine some factors that could be contributing to mental health problems (including differing forms of screen time)  
* Researchers primarily pulled from three long-term, national surveys administered to US adolescents: Monitoring the Future (MtF) (sample is 8th, 10th, and 12th graders, with data collected annually since 1991), Youth Risk Behavior Surveillance System (YRBSS) (administered to 9th-12th graders every other year since 1991), and CDC data (sample used is ages 13-18, from 1999 to 2015\) on suicide mortalities  
* The administered surveys assessed the following categories: depressive symptoms, suicide-related outcomes, electronic device use, social media use, Internet news, TV watching, in-person social interaction, print media, sports or exercise, religious services, paid job, demographic moderators and controls, and economic factors.  
* One major limitation of survey method is that, although there is a lot of data, there was no way to longitudinally track participants and look at long-term effects of certain activities on depressive symptoms and suicidal outcomes. Instead, the study analyzed concurrent effects  
    
* First, the researchers’ statistical analysis found that “depressive symptoms, suicide-related outcomes, and suicide deaths among adolescents all rose during the 2010s,” and the rise in depressive symptoms among female adolescents in particular (the main drivers of the overall change) 2010-2015 “corresponds to *d* \= 0.54 per year, an unusual amount of change for a short period of time”   
* Next, comparing adolescent activities they found that “**adolescents who spent more time on screen activities were significantly more likely to have high depressive symptoms or have at least one suicide-related outcome, and those who spent more time on nonscreen activities were less likely**”   
  * **Note** that **this is overall screen time**, not just SMU. To be fair, social media does take away adolescents’ time from nonscreen activities which were shown as more positive for mental health–but this does not correlate/implicate social media specifically as the problem  
* **New media screen activities** (**specifically electronic device use and social media**, rather than any screens like TVs) **had a higher correlation in their relationship to mental health in girls than boys**  
  * “**Social media use was significantly correlated with depressive symptoms among girls … but not among boys** … (with demographic controls)”   
  * Overall, “**adolescents using social media sites every day were 13% more likely to report high levels of depressive symptoms than those using social media less often**”   
* The suicide-related outcomes assessed by the study were also “all significantly correlated with electronic device use”; again, this is not only social media use, but that is definitely a large part of it. See previous bullet for social media-specific outcomes  
  * “Risks became elevated after 2 hours or more a day of electronic device use, when 33% of adolescents had at least one suicide-related outcome, compared to 29% of those using electronic devices 1 hour a day”   
    * By five hours, this likelihood increases to 66% more (compared to 1 hr/day)  
    * Provides supporting evidence to guidelines such as [those of the AACAP](screen-time-association-with-psychological-well-being.md), limiting but not removing screen time (& by extension social media use)  
* The researchers did also find that, for social media specifically, “**adolescents who spent more time interacting with friends in person also spent more time on social media**,” and in-person social interaction was correlated with SMU  
* **Controlling for this in-person social interaction, “the correlation between depressive symptoms and social media use increases … for girls and boys**”  
  * Shows that social media use can be a positive force/not have as many negative mental health effects if it enables/is related to frequent in-person interaction, but just social media use w/o interaction w/ friends has even more significant negative effects than the overall trend in the study  
  * Also suggests in-person interaction might have an offsetting effect on negative SMU effects: “**among those low in in-person social interaction, social media use had a significant effect on depressive symptoms … but among those high in in-person social interaction, social media did not have a significant effect**”   
* The study also considers the idea that economic hardship may be responsible for an increase in depressive/suicidal outcomes among adolescents. However, the authors concluded via statistical analysis that “the **mental health indicators were mostly unconnected to cyclical economic factors**,” and noted how **in contrast “the indicators of poor mental health rose in tandem with smartphone adoption”**  
  * Note this is not empirical evidence, just speculation, and also encompasses all of smartphone use rather than just SMU  
* Another factor considered was homework load, but “adolescents who spent more time on homework had lower depressive symptoms, in effect ruling homework out as a possible direct cause”  
  * Note: I am hesitant to agree with this claim fully, because this could be a biased sampling method towards mentally healthy individuals. Isn’t it more likely that someone mentally healthy will be able to spend more time focusing on homework, while someone less mentally healthy would probably have less energy/time to dedicate to it even if they have a high HW load? Would be curious to see how authors address this point  
* Significant **shortcoming of** this **paper**, noted by authors**: it is possible mental health issues in adolescents increased starting from 2011 for some reason other than smartphones** (or, most likely IMO, a complex combination of factors)--**and** that **depressed**/mentally unhealthy **adolescents were more drawn to screen time** and/or SMU than others  
  * Authors reference past papers as potential supporting indicators for causality relationship; definitely more research necessary, however  
    * The relationships noted in this study, regardless, do merit notice given how multiple likely factors besides screen time were discarded and screen time consistently correlated over the course of many years & individuals

**American Psychological Association Health Advisory on Social Media Use in Adolescence** (URL:[https://www.apa.org/topics/social-media-internet/health-advisory-adolescent-social-media-use.pdf](https://www.apa.org/topics/social-media-internet/health-advisory-adolescent-social-media-use.pdf))   
*(Published May 2023\)*

* SMU in adolescence is a “rapidly evolving and growing area of research with implications for many stakeholders (e.g., youth, parents, caregivers, educators, policymakers, practitioners, and members of the tech industry)...”  
* Recommendations below based on “scientific evidence to date (April 2023), and the following considerations”  
  * Using social media is not inherently beneficial or harmful to adolescents  
    * Life online both reflects and impacts offline life  
    * Effects of social media depend on what teens can do and see online, teens’ pre-existing strengths or vulnerabilities, and the contexts in which they grow up  
  * Experiences are affected by both 1\) how teens shape their own social media experiences (e.g., via likes and follows) and 2\) both visible and unknown features built into social media platforms  
  * Not all findings apply equally to all youth  
  * **Age-appropriate use of social media should be based on each adolescent’s level of maturity** (e.g., self regulation skills, intellectual development, comprehension of risks) and home environment   
    * Generally, potential risks likely to be greater in early adolescence (beginning around 10 years old, a period of greater biological, social, and psychological transitions) than late adolescence and early adulthood  
  * Racism is built into social media platforms (e.g., algorithms can have centuries of racist policy and discrimination encoded)  
    * Social media can become an incubator fueling racist hate  
    * Serious potential impacts can include physical violence offline and threats to well-being  
  * Studies referenced have limitations   
    * Findings suggesting casual associations are rare, as the data required to make cause-and-effect conclusions are challenging to collect and/or may be available within technology companies, but have not been made accessible to independent scientists   
    * Long-term research often unavailable, thus long-term outcomes of SMU (i.e., into adulthood) largely known  
    * Relatively few studies conducted with marginalized populations of youth

Recommendations 

* “Youth using social media should be encouraged to use functions that create opportunities for social support, online companionship, and emotional intimacy that can promote healthy socialization.”  
  * Youths’ psychological development may benefit from this type of online social interaction (particularly during times of social isolation, stress, or seeking connection to peers with similar developmental and/or health conditions, and especially for those who experience adversity or isolation offline)  
  * Youth with symptoms of mental illness (like social anxiety, depression, loneliness, etc.) could benefit from interactions on social media that allow greater control, practice, and review of social interactions (however, these populations are also probably more vulnerable to some of the negative facets of social media as well)  
  * Discussion of healthy/prosocial behaviors online can promote or reinforce positive offline activity and healthy outcome   
  * **May be psychologically beneficial particularly among those experiencing mental health crises or members of marginalized groups that have been disproportionately harmed in online contexts** (can protect youth from negative psychological outcomes when experiencing stress, especially important for topics that adolescents struggle with discussing with a parent or caregiver)  
* “Social media use, functionality, and permissions/consenting should be tailored to youths’ developmental capabilities; designs created for adults may not be appropriate for children”  
  * E.g., **adolescents should be informed explicitly and repeatedly, in age-appropriate ways, about the manner in which their behaviors on social media may yield data that can be used, stored, or shared with others**   
  * In early adolescence (typically 10-14 years), adult monitoring is advised for most youths’ social media use; autonomy may increase gradually as kids age and if they gain digital literacy skills   
    * Brain regions associated with desire for attention, feedback, and peer reinforcement become increasingly sensitive beginning early adolescence, and regions associated with mature self-control are not fully developed until adulthood (thus, parental monitoring during this time)  
  * Adults’ own use of social media in youths’ presence should be carefully considered  
    * Adults’ orientation and attitudes toward social media may affect adolescents’ own SMU)  
  * Preliminary research suggests that combination of social media limits and boundaries, and adult-child discussion and coaching around social media use, leads to best outcomes for youth  
* “To reduce the risks of psychological harm, adolescents’ exposure to content on social media that depicts illegal or psychologically maladaptive behavior, including content that instructs or encourages youth to engage in health-risk behaviors, such as self harm (e.g., cutting, suicide), harm to others, or those that encourage eating-disordered behavior (e.g., restrictive eating, purging, excessive exercise) should be minimized, reported, and removed; moreover, technology should not drive users to this content.”  
  * Exposure to maladaptive behavior may promote similar behavior among vulnerable youth, and online social reinforcement of these behaviors may be related to increased risk for serious psychological symptoms, even after controlling for offline influences   
  * Reporting structures should be created to easily identify/deprioritize/remove harmful content  
* “To minimize psychological harm, adolescents’ exposure to ‘cyberhate’ including online discrimination, prejudice, hate, or cyberbullying especially directed toward a marginalized group (e.g., racial, ethnic, gender, sexual, religious, ability status), or toward an individual because of their identity or allyship with a marginalized group should be minimized.”  
  * **Adolescents’ exposure to online discrimination/hate predicts increases in depressive/anxiety symptoms**, even after controlling for how much adolescents are exposed to similar experiences offline  
  * Compared to offline bullying, online bullying/harassment can be more severe, and thus damaging to psychological development (posing elevated risks both for the perpetrators and victims of cyberhate)  
  * Adolescents should be trained to recognize online structural racism and critique racist messages – young people who are able to critique racism experience less psychological distress when they witness race-related traumatic events online  
* “Adolescents should be routinely screened for signs of “problematic social media use” that can impair their ability to engage in daily roles and routines, and may present risk for more serious psychological harms over time.”  
  * **Indicators of problematic social media use include a) tendency to use social media even when adolescents want to stop, or realize it is interfering with necessary tasks; b) spending excessive effort to ensure continuous access to social media; c) strong cravings to use social media, or disruptions in other activities from missing social media use too much; d) repeatedly spending more time on social media than intended; e) lying or deceptive behavior to retain access to social media use; f) loss or disruption of significant relationships or educational opportunities because of social media use**  
  * Social media shouldn’t restrict opportunities for in-person reciprocal social interactions, and shouldn’t contribute to psychological avoidance of in-person social interactions  
* “The use of social media should be limited so as to not interfere with adolescents’ sleep and physical activity.”  
  * Adolescents should ideally get at least 8 hours of sleep each night & maintain regular sleep-wake schedules  
  * Tech use particularly within 1 hour of bedtime, and social media use in particular, is associated with sleep disruptions  
  * **Insufficient sleep is associated with disruptions to neurological development in adolescent brains, teens’ emotional functioning, and risk for suicide**  
  * Adolescents’ social media use shouldn’t interfere with/reduce their opportunities for physical activity and exercise – physical activity is essential for both physical and psychological health (i.e., lower rates of depression)  
* “Adolescents should limit use of social media for social comparison, particularly around beauty- or appearance-related content.”  
  * Using social media for social comparisons related to physical appearance, and excessive attention to behaviors related to one’s own photos and feedback on those photos, are related to poorer body image, disordered eating, and depressive symptoms, particularly among girls  
* “Adolescents’ social media use should be preceded by training in social media literacy to ensure that users have developed psychologically-informed competencies and skills that will maximize the chances for balanced, safe, and meaningful social media use.”  
  * Emerging science offers preliminary support for the efficacy of Digital Citizenship and Digital Literacy to increase the frequency of positive interactions online; however, more research is needed in this area  
  * **Additional competencies could also include: 1\) questioning the accuracy and representativeness of social media content; 2\) understanding the tactics used to spread mis- and disinformation; 3\) limiting “overgeneralization” and “misestimation” errors that lead users to incorrectly estimate others’ behaviors or attitudes based on social media content (or reactions to content); 4\) signs of problematic social media use; 5\) how to build and nourish healthy online relationships; 6\) how to solve conflicts that can emerge on social media platforms; 7\) how to refrain from excessive social comparisons online and/or better understand how images and content can be manipulated; 8\) as noted above, how to recognize online structural racism and critique racist messages; and 9\) how to safely communicate about mental health online**  
* “Substantial resources should be provided for continued scientific examination of the positive and negative effects of social media on adolescent development.”  
  * Substantial investment in research funding needed, including long-term longitudinal research, studies of younger children, and research on marginalized populations  
  * Access to data among independent scientists (including data from tech companies) to more thoroughly examine associations between SMU and adolescent development needed
